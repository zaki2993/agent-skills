# PROVOKE: tools to escape a pattern

Source: Edward de Bono, *Lateral Thinking*.

**Why these work:** a mind (and a model) stores ideas as patterns. Patterns get deeper the more they are used, and they control where attention goes. A pattern cannot be restructured from inside itself. It needs a push from outside, and "reasonable" pushes are too close to the old pattern to shift it. So these tools are deliberately unreasonable. You use information for its **effect** (where it leads), not for its truth. Judge later.

Pick any tool. Spend a few minutes on it. Write down what comes out, even half-ideas.

## 1. Ask "why?" when you already know the answer

Normal "why" seeks comfort. This one creates discomfort with every explanation.

- Point it at one specific part: "Why is this a server?" "Why does this step happen before that one?" "Why this format?" "Why do we need this at all?"
- Never accept "because that's how it's done". The reason something started is not a reason to continue.
- **The "cheating!" test:** if a solution would feel like cheating, you just found a boundary you set yourself. Most boundaries are self-imposed for convenience. (Nine dots: the lines may leave the square. Four equidistant trees: plant one on a hill.)
- Re-examine the pieces, not only how they are arranged. Sometimes the fix is to split a piece everyone treats as one unit.

## 2. Reversal

Take what is there and flip it: direction, order, who acts on whom, inside-out, time backwards. Any reversal will do; you are not looking for the "correct" one. See where it leads.

- A shepherd blocking a car did not move the sheep aside. He stopped the car and walked the flock back past it.
- A crow could not reach the water. It added pebbles instead of taking water out.
- A 111-player knockout tournament: count the losers, not the winners. 110 losers, so 110 matches.
- Ask: what if the data came to the code? What if the client did the server's job? What if we ran it last instead of first? What if the user produced it instead of us?

## 3. PO: say something wrong on purpose

PO marks a statement as a provocation: "I know this is wrong; let's see where it leads." It moves attention from "why is this wrong" to "what is useful in it".

Ways to use it:
- **Escort through a wrong area:** "PO drive the car backwards" (nonsense, since the back window gets dirty too) leads to indirect vision and protecting the front from head-on dirt.
- **Turn a NO into a PO:** every hard "can't" marks the current frame. "PO you can live if your heart stops" leads to artificial hearts and new definitions of death.
- **Add something odd to the problem:** "PO the police are one-armed" puts the focus on brains and organization over muscle. A lawyer split 11 horses (½, ¼, ⅙) by lending one horse, splitting 12, and taking his horse back.
- **Juxtapose two unrelated things:** "compiler PO garden". Do not look for a reason. Look at what comes out.
- **Challenge a concept, not its use:** "PO tests" or "PO users" questions the concept itself.

Rule: a provocation is never a final answer. It is a stepping stone. Once a new idea snaps into place, judge it with full rigor.

## 4. Random entry

Pick a truly random word. You must not choose it, because choosing means it fits your current view, which defeats the purpose.

```bash
shuf -i 1-60 -n 1   # then use that number from the list below
```

Hold the word next to the problem for about three minutes. Follow chains (word → related word → link to problem), its functions and properties, even puns. Do not skip to another word if the first feels useless; switching turns it into a search for a "fitting" word. Any two things held together will connect.

Example from the book: "noose" + "housing shortage" gave bottleneck analysis, houses hung from a shared frame, expandable round houses, and swappable standard housing units.

## 5. Fractionation: break it into fake parts

The more unified a pattern is, the harder it is to change. A doll's house can only be admired; a box of blocks can become anything.

- Split the problem into parts. Do **not** look for the "true" parts; true parts snap back into the original design. Invent artificial ones. Overlap and gaps are fine.
- If stuck, split in two, then split each half in two again.
- Recombine differently. (Apple picking: reach + find + pick merged into "shake the tree". Splitting by who contributes, tree vs. apple vs. machine, led to "grow the trees differently".)

## 6. Climb the function ladder

The more specific the description of what you need, the more it traps you.

- "Pick the apples" → "remove the apples" → "separate apples from tree" → "get apples where we want them." Each step up allows new answers (shake the tree, grow it in a shape that drops fruit onto a belt).
- Or flip it: "remove the tree from around the apples."
- Generate different **functions**, not just different ways to do one function. A cup that won't spill can be one that can't fall over, or one that doesn't spill when it falls.

## 7. Change where you start and what you look at

The order of attention decides the conclusion, even with the same facts.

- **Start from the end.** Begin with the finished result and work back. (Tangled fishing lines: trace from the fish to the rod, not from the rods.) If there is no clear start point, invent one.
- **Look at what everyone ignores.** The losers, the empty space, the dog that did nothing in the night, the path nobody uses, the side effect. A drug's "side effect" of more urine became a useful drug for exactly that.
- **Rotate attention.** List every feature of the situation, including the trivial ones, and spend real attention on each. What is left out never comes back by itself, and nothing in front of you shows what is missing.
- **Put back what was left out.** Every description drops information. Ask what the story omits.
- **Watch the effects of the fix itself.** Spraying weeds lowered crop yield. Burning infected carcasses spread the virus in the smoke.

## 8. Name the dominant idea and the crucial factors

- **Dominant idea:** the theme that organizes how a situation is seen. Children designing apple pickers thought "reach the apples". Engineers thought "beat the cost of hand labor". Name yours in one sentence, then design as if it were false.
- **Crucial factor:** something that "must" be included in any solution. It is often just an assumption. Ask: what is holding us to the old approach? Remove it and see what opens up.

## 9. Drop the label

Labels freeze ideas. Once something is named, the name shapes what you see.

- Describe the problem without its key word ("cache", "user", "server", "test", "model"). Whatever you are forced to write instead is a new way of seeing it.
- Watch for forced categories: things pushed into one of two boxes (sync/async, client/server, build/buy) when they fit neither.
- Give a new idea a new name, so the baggage of the old word does not shape it.

## 10. Trim the borrowed parts

People borrow whole units from elsewhere (a robot apple picker with a five-fingered hand). For each borrowed part:
- **Trim** what is not needed. Keep cutting until only the essential piece is left. (From a tank, keep only the caterpillar track.)
- **Extract** the function and find other ways to do it.
- **Combine** parts from different sources into something that exists nowhere.

## 11. Add a hard constraint

Limits push past the easy answers. "Do it in two cuts" made people find the diagonal. Try: zero dependencies; 1/100 of the compute; in 50 lines; with no network; in a different language; with no database; in one hour; with only what existed 20 years ago.

## 12. Extract the kernel from bad ideas

Never just reject. Ask: "This won't work, but what does it lead to?"
- Magnets buried under apple trees (absurd) → kernel: bring all the apples down at once instead of one by one.
- A cart that lays "smooth stuff" ahead and picks it up behind (absurd) → kernel: change the ground, not the wheels → tracked vehicles.
- Trains with tracks on their roofs (absurd) → kernel: use the same track twice.

## 13. Restate the problem

Write the problem 3 to 5 different ways, at different levels. Too wide ("better traffic") gives scattered ideas. Too narrow ("better traffic lights") locks you into one solution's details. Aim between ("better traffic flow on the roads we already have"). Each restatement suggests different answers.

## The attitude behind all of these

- The only thing wrong with an existing way is the rigid certainty it is held with.
- You can be wrong along the way, as long as you are right at the end. Sometimes you must pass through a wrong position to see the right path. Once you reach the answer, you can build the logical path back to the start.
- In hindsight, every good new idea looks obvious and "logical". That does not mean logic would have found it going forward.
- A challenge is not an attack. "PO" means "you may be right, but let's look for other ways." Answer it by generating more alternatives, not by defending.

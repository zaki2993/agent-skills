# RANGE: borrow from far away, judge bets honestly

Source: David Epstein, *Range*.

**Why this matters:** breakthroughs rarely come from digging the same hole deeper. They come from knowledge that is outside the usual curriculum, from analogies to distant fields, from old tools used in new ways, and from people who are not trapped by the field's habits. Breadth also makes judgment better: people with one big theory get *worse* as they learn more, because they bend every fact to fit it.

## 1. Kind vs. wicked problems

- **Kind:** the rules are clear, patterns repeat, and feedback is fast and honest (chess, golf, a well-tested build). Experience and the standard method work. Don't over-think it.
- **Wicked:** the rules are unclear or changing, patterns may not repeat, and feedback is slow or misleading. Here experience mostly builds confidence, not skill. A famous doctor kept "confirming" typhoid by touching patients' tongues and was in fact spreading it. Every "success" taught him the wrong lesson.
- Experts in a kind field can do worse than beginners once the rules change slightly (bridge with a changed order of play, accountants with a new tax law). This is called **cognitive entrenchment**. Your pattern-matching is the same: it is great on familiar problems and brittle on new ones.

## 2. Use many distant analogies

- **Kepler** had no data and no concept of gravity. Each time he got stuck, he went through analogies: smell, heat, light, magnets, whirlpools, boatmen, brooms. He questioned each one hard. That is how he invented physical laws for the sky.
- **Duncker's radiation problem:** about 10% solve it alone. Given one story from another domain with the same structure (an army splitting up to converge on a fortress), about 30% solve it. Given two, about 50%. Told to use them, about 80%. People rarely use analogies on their own, even when they are right in front of them.
- **More and farther is better.** Business students reminded of Nike and McDonald's produced more strategies for a computer-mouse company than those reminded of Apple and Dell. Yet people instinctively choose one close example from the same field, which is the wrong way.
- Match on **deep structure** (feedback loop, bottleneck, matching problem, diffusion, auction), not on surface (same industry, same tech).
- **How:** name the deep structure of your problem in one line with no domain words. List 3 to 5 far fields that face the same structure. Ask how each one solves it. Map their solution back step by step.

## 3. The outsider's advantage

- On InnoCentive, problems that stumped specialist teams were often solved by outsiders, and **the further the problem was from the solver's own field, the more likely they were to solve it.** A lawyer solved a chemistry problem by thinking of tear gas.
- An oil-spill chemist dropped chemistry ("adding chemicals to a chemical problem is bad"), thought of drinking a slushy, then remembered one day on a building site: a concrete vibrator keeps concrete fluid. That was the solution, written in three pages.
- A canning pioneer (a confectioner, brewer, and pickle maker) solved food preservation where scientists had failed for a century, 60 years before anyone knew why it worked.
- **Einstellung effect:** solvers keep using the familiar method even when a better one is available. Specialists get stuck in nested boxes: escape one and you are inside the next.
- **How to use it:** ask how a person from a totally different job would attack this (a plumber, a game designer, a biologist, a logistics planner, a teacher). Strip the jargon from the problem so an outsider could understand it. That restatement alone often unlocks it.
- **When experts say "impossible",** look again. One 3M inventor said that if experts tell you "great idea, go for it", the chance you are the first is zero. Experts know their field but not "the adjacent stuff". But "impossible because of physics" is different from "impossible because nobody does it".

## 4. Old tech, new use

- **Gunpei Yokoi** (Nintendo) called it "lateral thinking with withered technology": use old, cheap, well-understood tech in ways nobody considered. An RC car that only turns left cost a tenth of the price. A calculator screen became the Game & Watch. The Game Boy had an outdated chip and four shades of grey, and it crushed color rivals because it was cheap, tough, ran for days on batteries, and developers already knew the hardware.
- "If we can't think more deeply about new tech, we can think more broadly about old tech." His biggest failure came when he dropped this idea for experimental tech.
- A 4th-century recipe led to a malaria drug after 240,000 modern compounds had failed. A childhood memory of shirt starch gave gel electrophoresis. Scotch tape on pencil graphite gave graphene.
- **Functional fixedness:** we see objects only in their usual use. In the candle problem, people solve it more easily once the tacks are taken out of their box, because then the box can become a shelf.
- **How:** list the boring, mature, "solved" tools near the problem. Ask what each could do here if you ignored what it is "for". Ask what the stripped-down minimum version would be.

## 5. Drop your familiar tools

- Firefighters died running uphill still holding their saws and packs. Their tools were their identity, and dropping them felt like "forgetting you are a firefighter".
- NASA's strong data culture ("bring data") silenced engineers whose real concern (photos of soot, "away from goodness") could not be put into numbers. Under pressure, experienced groups **regress to what they know best** and bend a new situation into an old one.
- Specialties built around one tool see that tool's problem everywhere (blocked artery → stent, even where trials show no benefit).
- **Ask yourself:** what tool or method do I always reach for? What if it were not available? Is the data I'm given the data this decision needs? Is the missing data the important data?
- **Hunches held lightly:** give a clear direction and a clear reason, but treat the plan as something you will revise. "A decision is something I own and defend. Sense-making is something I can change."

## 6. Foxes beat hedgehogs

- In a 20-year study (Tetlock), experts with one big theory (**hedgehogs**) predicted about as well as chance, and got *worse* with more information. They counted wins as proof and losses as "near misses".
- **Foxes** pull from many sources, accept contradiction, and combine opposing views. When something surprises them, they change their mind. Amateur "superforecasters" with broad interests beat intelligence analysts who had classified data.
- **Active open-mindedness:** treat your own idea as a hypothesis and look for what would prove it wrong. When a result is the opposite of what you expected, read it as "my model is wrong", not "the data is weird".
- Take **facts** from specialists, not their opinions.

## 7. Judge bets honestly

- **Outside view:** the more details you know about your own plan, the more optimistic and extreme your guess becomes. Find a reference class of similar cases and start from how those usually went. One team estimated 2 years; similar projects took 7 or more, and 40% never finished. It took them 8.
- Use a whole set of analogies for estimates, not the single "best" one. Predictions from a single best match collapse.
- **Breakthroughs are high variance.** The most creative people make the most duds and the biggest hits. "Breakthrough and fallacy look a lot alike at first."
- **Work that combines unusual knowledge** gets less funding and is ignored at first, then becomes the most cited. Hit work is mostly conventional, plus a dose of unusual combinations.
- **Set quit conditions before you start.** Decide in advance what result means "switch". Without it, sunk cost keeps you on a dead path.
- **Fast hints kill deep learning.** When struggling, don't turn a "why does this work?" problem into a recipe too soon. Short-term ease often means long-term failure.

## 8. Test-and-learn, not plan-and-implement

- Michelangelo did not "see the statue in the marble". He changed plans constantly and left most works unfinished, moving to whatever was more promising.
- Don't work back from a fixed long-term goal. Work forward from the most promising options available now, and choose moves that keep the most good options open later. (Paul Graham calls fixed early plans "premature optimization".)
- **Friday-night / Saturday-morning experiments:** small, cheap, slightly irrational tries outside the main work. Most produce nothing; a few produce Nobel prizes. "Be careful not to be too careful" (the principle of limited sloppiness).
- Bring new skills to an old problem, or an old problem to new skills. Ideas that are ordinary in one field can be inventions in another.
